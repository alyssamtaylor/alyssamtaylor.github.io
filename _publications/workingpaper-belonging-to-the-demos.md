## Working Abstract

While migration research centered in developed countries has focused largely on attitudes toward immigrants, we know little about how citizens view emigrants, in particular their access to “citizen rights”. This paper examines the extent to which resident citizens regard those who have left as still belonging to the national demos, and what conditions can lead to their disenfranchisement. Drawing on a preregistered survey experiment fielded in Switzerland, Sweden, and the United States (US) in late 2024, we test criteria drawn from both legal statute (e.g., length of residency abroad) and transnational scholarship (e.g., interest in home-country politics, social ties) to understand boundaries around belonging to the national polity. We further consider whether two particular types of emigrants: those born abroad to emigrants and dual nationals are penalized. First, we find that markers of connection to the home country—recency of departure, intention to return, and prior political participation—are associated with resident citizens’ willingness to maintain co-citizens’ national voting rights, whereas ascriptive characteristics are not, and emigrants born abroad are penalized relative to those who left themselves. Second, we consider what could be driving citizens’ boundary-making. We find respondents’ national identities, their political orientation, as well as their own migration histories, influence overall support for inclusion, but have little impact on who they include or exclude.
